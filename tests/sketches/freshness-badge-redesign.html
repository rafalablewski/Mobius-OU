Tests · Freshness-badge R&D · 2026.Q2

Fifteen redesigns of the "UPDATED 2026.Q1" badge.

The current freshness indicator (gold pulsing dot + caps label) does the job, but reads as a generic SaaS "live" pill — slightly at odds with the editorial, advisory register of the rest of the program page. Three rounds below. Round one (01–05) is the pragmatic set. Round two (06–10) is bolder and more decorative — wax seal, watermark, postage stamp, redline, almanac. Round three (11–15) dials all the way back: institutional, restrained, mostly mono and hairlines — audit-report register. Each is shown as a pencil sketch and a live HTML mock on dark ground so we can compare the actual rendering, not just the idea.

★ Direction agreed: Idea 11 (document-control block) is the favoured route, refined so the first cell tags the program category — RBI or CBI — and the doc code becomes a real country-program slug rather than an opaque string. See ↓ Idea 11 (refined) for both variants and the convention applied across all six current programs.

Idea 01

A dossier stamp, not a status pill.

Reads as if a partner has personally signed the file off this quarter. Faded gold ink, subtle rotation, double hairline border — the language of an archives folder, not a product changelog. Works because the rest of the page is already styled like a brief. Stamp ink is opacity-reduced and slightly textured so it never feels skeuomorphic-cute.

Versus today: the pulsing dot says "this is a live system." A stamp says "this file has been reviewed." Better fit with NDA-first tone. Cost: it's the most ornamental option and has to be executed with restraint or it tips into kitsch.

Idea 02

Drop the badge entirely — run a masthead line.

Lose the box. Replace it with a single typographic line under the breadcrumb, magazine- masthead style: Issue 14 · Q1 2026 · Revised 04 May. Hairline below. Says the same thing with more authority and zero visual noise — and lets us start treating each program page like an ongoing publication ("Issue n") rather than a static landing page.

Versus today: calmer, more editorial, more information per pixel — and it sets up an "Issue" framing we can re-use in the briefs index, the changelog, and the partner-only updates email. Cost: it carries less weight at-a-glance for users who only scan; it loses the "is this current?" pop the gold dot gives.

Idea 03

A changelog tab that opens a side drawer.

Same footprint as today, but the badge becomes a click target. Users who care ("is this still current?") get a drawer listing the three most recent revisions — fee schedule bumped, processing window updated, residency rules clarified — each timestamped and signed by a partner initial. Users who don't care just see a tidier label. Doubles as proof we actively maintain the page.

Versus today: turns a passive label into an answer to the implicit question "what changed?" That's the only question the freshness indicator is really trying to address. Cost: needs a new changelog field on each program in src/data/programs.js and a small drawer component.

Idea 04

A gutter mark rotated along the page edge.

Move the freshness signal off the breadcrumb entirely and onto the left edge of the hero, like the gutter notation on a printed ledger or chapter-rule on a hardcover spine. Vertical, rotated 90°, hairline-bordered. Frees up the meta row to focus purely on region/category. Creates a strong, almost architectural framing for the hero title.

Versus today: visually the most distinctive — instantly differentiates us from competitors who all use the same horizontal "updated" pill. Cost: rotated text is mildly less accessible; needs explicit aria-label, and we have to make sure it doesn't collide with the existing `.ht-breadcrumb__stage` aside on `ProgramPage.jsx:110`.

04 — Architectural. Most differentiating. Mind accessibility.

Idea 05

Soft "current" pill that earns its space on hover.

Closest to today, smallest delta, biggest payoff per minute of work. Replace the all-caps "UPDATED 2026.Q1" with a quieter outlined pill reading Current · Q1 2026 plus an info dot. On hover/focus a small tooltip appears: who reviewed it, when, and a one-line summary of what changed — with a link to the full quarterly note in the briefs index.

Versus today: same rough silhouette so it doesn't break the existing breadcrumb layout, but it now communicates "reviewed" instead of "updated" — language that sits better next to the rest of the advisory voice — and provides a low-cost path to deeper proof for users who want it. Best risk/reward of the five.

Round two · 06 — 10

Five more, bolder.

Round one stayed close to the existing language of the page. Round two pushes harder on register: a wax-seal signet, a chapter-mark watermark, a passport-style postage stamp, an editor's hand-drawn correction, and an almanac-style roman-numeral plate. Each is more risk and more reward — they only work if executed with discipline, but any one of them could become the page's signature flourish.

Idea 06

A wax-seal monogram — the page's signet ring.

A small circular seal in the top-right of the hero: pressed gold wax, embossed Mobius monogram in italic Fraunces, "Q1 · MMXXVI · MOBIUS" running along the rim in a thin sans. Reads instantly as ceremony and provenance — the visual language of consular seals, notarised documents and old correspondence. Sits perfectly in a citizenship- and-residency context.

Versus today: trades a generic "live" pill for an object that says "this dossier was sealed this quarter." Strongest brand-asset payoff of all ten — the seal itself can spread to the briefs index, partner emails, even physical letterhead. Cost: most production work; needs a clean SVG asset and care that it doesn't read as "wedding invitation." Restraint required.

06 — A signet ring for the page. Strongest brand asset of the ten.

Idea 07

A giant "Q1" watermark behind the title — a chapter mark.

Set "Q1" as a 150-pt italic Fraunces in 8% gold opacity, anchored to the right of the hero behind the title. Reads first as an aesthetic flourish; reads second as the freshness signal. Echoes the way novels and journals open chapters with a giant drop-numeral. Pairs with a small "MMXXVI · REVISED 04 MAY" caption underneath the lede so the literal information is still legible.

Versus today: most atmospheric option — turns the "updated" signal into a piece of typography rather than a chip. Reinforces the periodical framing every program page is reaching for. Cost: pure decoration, doesn't communicate at a glance to a scanner; needs the small caption to pull its weight.

07 — A chapter mark, not a chip. Most atmospheric.

Idea 08

A postage stamp with a Q1 cancellation mark.

Perforated edges, inner gold-rule frame, "MOBIUS" in small caps along the top, italic "Q1 · 2026" centred, "REV. 04.05" underneath. Overlaid: a small circular cancellation stamp tilted at -9°, "Q1 · 2026" rolling across the corner — exactly like a passport entry stamp. Most thematic option of the ten: directly invokes the visual language of international travel and citizenship.

Versus today: turns the freshness mark into a passport-stamp motif that's literally on-theme for the entire site. Memorable, ownable, easy to reuse on brief PDFs and partner letterhead. Cost: technically the trickiest to render (perforations need either CSS masks or an SVG); slightly skeuomorphic, so registration and ink colour have to stay restrained.

Idea 09

An editor's correction — old quarter struck through, new one written in.

Show the old period struck through in redline (Q4 2025) and the new one (Q1 2026) written next to it, with a small Caveat-handwriting margin note — "rev. R.A." Looks like an editor has personally amended the file. Most human and irreverent option: the only one that signals not just "this was updated" but "a real person updated it, on purpose, and didn't bother hiding the seam."

Versus today: by far the most distinctive voice — nothing else in our space looks anything like this. Doubles as a quiet brag (we revise often enough that revisions are a feature, not a footnote). Cost: it's a stylistic statement; would feel odd next to a lifeless hero, so it's only worth doing if rest of the page also leans into the "editor's desk" register.

Idea 10

An almanac plate — Roman numerals between two hairlines.

Two close-set gold hairlines (one heavier, one lighter — the classic "scotch rule" from old almanacs and quarterly journals). Between them, Roman numerals in italic Fraunces: MMXXVI · Q I. Above, four asterisk-spaced stars. Below, "REVISED IV · V" set in tiny tracked caps. Reads as the cover plate of a quarterly bulletin — timeless, mythic, slightly archival. The most "publication" of the ten.

Versus today: trades immediate scannability for a deeper sense of institutional weight — this looks like something the firm publishes, not something the website automatically renders. Pairs naturally with the masthead-line idea (02) and the watermark (07) if we ever consolidate. Cost: roman numerals slow first parse; mitigate by always pairing with a small Arabic caption.

10 — A quarterly-journal cover plate. Most "publication" of the ten.

Round three · 11 — 15

Five more, institutional.

Round two was too ornamental. These five take the opposite stance: no decoration, no animation, no gold dot, mostly tabular monospace and hairlines. Reference points are the footer of an audit report, the dateline of the FT, the version line at the bottom of an SEC filing — not anything that looks designed. The page should be the thing that's interesting; the freshness signal should sit out of the way and just tell you the truth.

Idea 11 · refined

A document-control block — front-cell tags RBI or CBI.

The first cell is the program category — RBI (residency by investment) or CBI (citizenship by investment) — set in a small framed tag. That's what readers actually want to know first; everything else is reference. Then the country- program code (PT-GV, MT-MEIN, CARIB …), the ISO review date, and the confidentiality classification. The previous mock used PT-GV-14 with a trailing version number — meaningless on its own, and duplicative with the date. Dropped.

Versus the first pass: the row now answers "what kind of program is this?" before it answers "what's the file number?" — which matches how a partner would actually read it across the desk. The convention also forces us to commit to a clean country-program code per file in src/data/programs.js, side by side with the existing category field.

11 — RBI/CBI is the headline; the doc code is reference, not decoration.

Idea 12

An ISO datestamp. One line. Tabular numerals. Done.

Strip everything off. A single line under the breadcrumb meta: REVIEWED  2026-05-04 in tabular Manrope. Optional second cell on the right with the next scheduled review (NEXT  Q3-2026). One hairline above. No badge, no chip, no color. The most professional thing on the page is sometimes a dateline.

Versus today: reads as if it came out of a publishing system, not a design system. Loses zero information; loses all the visual noise. Bonus: ISO 8601 is unambiguous across our cross-border audience in a way "Q1" and "May 4" aren't. Cost: the calmest of all fifteen — easy to overlook entirely.

12 — A dateline, not a design.

Idea 13

A small filing-reference table — three label/value cells.

Three columns separated by hairlines: REVIEWED / VERSION / NEXT REVIEW, with values 04 May 2026 · 14.1 · Q3 2026. Reads as the header of a financial statement or the metadata table on the cover page of a research report. Three pieces of information for the price of one badge, and the table format itself signals "this is maintained on a schedule."

Versus today: the badge is one fact ("updated this quarter"); this is three facts in the same footprint, and one of them — next review date — is implicitly a commitment. Cost: occupies more horizontal space than the current badge; needs to slot into the breadcrumb meta row carefully so it doesn't crowd the region/category cells.

Idea 14

A semver tag — v.14.1 and a date. Engineering-grade.

Borrow the language of git tags and academic preprints: a small monospace v.14.1 in a thin outlined box, followed by Q1 2026 in tracked caps. Optional small "changelog →" link in muted ivory. Reads as published- research / open-source discipline rather than marketing copy — which is the actual register we want users to associate with our research.

Versus today: conveys "this is on revision N, not just 'fresh'" — stronger signal of methodical maintenance. Needs least net-new infrastructure of the three: a version string per program in src/data/programs.js, bumped on edit. Risk: leans engineering; doesn't match the tone if every other element on the page is in italic Fraunces.

14 — Open-source discipline. Pairs with an actual version field.

Idea 15

A standfirst — a single sentence under the meta, no badge.

Delete the badge entirely. Add one italic Fraunces line under the breadcrumb meta: "Last reviewed 4 May 2026; supersedes all prior versions." Set against a thin left rule. Reads as the standfirst under a leader article in the FT or the cover note of a legal opinion. The freshness signal becomes a sentence, and the sentence carries an implicit warranty ("supersedes all prior versions") that no chip or pill could.

Versus today: trades a chip for a quiet, on-the-record statement. Most "advisory voice" of all fifteen and the only one that says something stronger than "current". Cost: literally the most subtle option — easy to skip past on first scan; gains weight only if a user is already reading carefully.
