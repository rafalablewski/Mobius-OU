Five ways our program page can out-class Astons.

Astons leans on a saturated beach photo, a "largest agent" badge and a loud red consultation form pinned to the hero. We're playing a different game — editorial, advisory, NDA-first. These five moves keep our calm and add the conversion paths we're currently missing.

Idea 01

A discreet "Brief a Partner" panel on the hero — not a shouty form.

Astons drops a four-field form straight onto the hero, country code dropdown and all. It works for retail leads, but it cheapens the page. We give the same conversion path as a small, glass-card panel anchored to the right of the hero — three fields, NDA-first microcopy, gold button, no phone number required.

Versus Astons: they say "Receive a Consultation." We say "Brief us — we respond under NDA within two business days." Same conversion intent, opposite register.

01 — Brief panel sits inside the hero, not on top of it.

Idea 02

Replace the "largest agent" badge with a headline-figure ribbon.

Astons pushes a vague credibility tile ("The largest agent · official partner of Vanuatu"). Specifics outrank superlatives. We run a hairline-bordered ribbon directly under the hero with four hard numbers — minimum allocation, presence requirement, time-to-PR, and a firm stat (jurisdictions advised). Numbers count up on scroll-in.

Versus Astons: "largest agent" is unverifiable. €500,000 / 7 days / 5 years is unarguable. Hard numbers also do double duty as SEO answer-box bait.

02 — A hairline ribbon of four hard numbers sits flush under the hero.

Idea 03

Lean further into the intel-brief aesthetic — dossier stamp + watermark crest.

Our hero already has the right tone, but the dark right half is dead space. Fill it with a faint country crest watermark (≈8% opacity), and add a small rotated "PROGRAM DOSSIER · 2026.Q1" stamp in the top-right. Reads like a private-bank research note. Astons reads like a tour brochure.

Versus Astons: they fill the hero with a saturated beach photo. We do the opposite — restraint, gravitas, watermark instead of stock photo.

03 — Dossier stamp top-right, country crest watermark, "filed by" footer.

Idea 04

A named partner pull-quote directly under the hero.

Astons shows zero faces — just a beach and a form. Our edge is judgement, so lead with it. Below the hero, a band with a small portrait, the partner's name and role, and one sharp line: "Portugal still works — but only if you can stomach the AIMA queue." That single sentence does more than a paragraph of marketing copy.

Versus Astons: they sell the destination. We sell the call you're going to have. The quote is also the SEO snippet Google will pull.

04 — Portrait + name + one-line judgement = social proof we actually have.

Idea 05

A sticky compare bar at the bottom of the viewport.

Most clients reading a Portugal page are also looking at Greece, Spain and Malta. Don't make them go back to /pricing. A thin, persistent bar at the bottom of the viewport: "Compare: Greece · Spain · Malta" pills on the left, "Schedule Consultation" button on the right. Stays out of the way, always one click from the next surface.

Versus Astons: they keep their consultation form sticky on the side. We keep the comparison shelf sticky instead — that's what HNW clients are actually doing in their heads.

05 — Comparison shelf sticky on the bottom, never more than one click away.

Five ways to land the "Brief a Partner" panel.

Idea 01 is the right move — give the hero a conversion path without resorting to Astons' shouty red form. But "small panel anchored right" is one of many shapes it can take. Below, five genuinely distinct patterns. Pick one, A/B two, or layer them by breakpoint.

Baseline (the original 01 above): right-anchored glass card on the hero, three fields (you / jurisdiction / situation), gold "Send brief" button, microcopy "NDA-first · response in 48h · no marketing." Each variation below trades something against that baseline — different friction, different intent, different visual weight.
Variation 01·A

An inline conversational strip directly under the tagline.

No card, no panel — just a single rounded textarea sitting under the tagline like a private-message field. Placeholder reads "Tell us the situation in one sentence…" Gold arrow button on the right. Submitting opens a brief modal for name + email. Lower friction than three fields, conversational instead of formal.

Trade vs baseline: faster to start typing, less qualifying data up front. Best when the bottleneck is "they bounced before reaching the contact page," worst when the inbox needs pre-qualified leads.

01·A — One field, no card. Reads like a DM, not a form.

Variation 01·B

An inverted offer — give the dossier, ask for an email.

Flip the conversion logic. Instead of asking the visitor to brief us, we offer something tangible: "Get our 4-page private read on Portugal — minimums, real timelines, what we'd actually advise. Drop your email; the dossier and a calendar link arrive in your inbox." One field, big gold button, secondary link to schedule directly.

Trade vs baseline: commits us to producing the dossier (good content hygiene anyway), and inverts the energy — we're being generous, they're not yet committing. Probably the highest-converting variant; best for top-of-funnel traffic.

01·B — Inverts the ask. Probably the highest-converting variant.

Variation 01·C

A collapsed pill that expands into the full panel on tap.

Hero stays editorial-pure: just title, tagline, gold-rule. A small gold-bordered pill sits in the top-right corner — "Brief a Partner ↗". Click and it expands gracefully into the full three-field card (or animates open as a side drawer). Conversion path is one tap away without ever competing with the typography.

Trade vs baseline: you keep the cleanest possible hero, at the cost of one extra click. Probably the best fit if our north-star metric is "hero looks like an FT longread, not a landing page."

01·C — Collapsed pill (left) → expanded card (right). Cleanest hero.

Variation 01·D

A dock-on-scroll advisor card — appears once the hero is past.

Hero stays absolutely clean. The conversion path arrives later — once the user has scrolled past the hero, a compact card slides in from the bottom-right with a small partner portrait and one line: "Working on Portugal? Brief Filip directly →" Stays docked through the rest of the page. Marries idea 01 and idea 04 (named partner) into one element.

Trade vs baseline: the hero is pristine, but the conversion only appears for engaged readers. Skips drive-by traffic on purpose — fine if our buyer reads before they convert.

01·D — Conversion path docks in only when the reader is engaged.

Variation 01·E

A calendar-first panel — three real slots, no form.

Skip the form entirely. The card lists three actually-available slots from a partner's calendar — "Thu 7 May · 14:00 CET / Fri 8 May · 09:30 CET / Mon 11 May · 11:00 CET" — each tile clickable. Picking one opens a name+email modal and books the call. "Or pick another time →" link below as the escape hatch.

Trade vs baseline: zero typing to start; signals "we're real advisors with calendars, not a CRM." Costs us partner-time visibility and a Cal.com / Google integration. Best signal of quality bar; worst if a slot books with a tire-kicker.

01·E — Calendar slots replace the form. Maximum signal of quality bar.

How to choose between the variations: 01·A and 01·E minimise typing; 01·B inverts the offer and likely converts highest; 01·C protects the typography; 01·D respects the reader. Worth shipping two and A/B-testing — start with the baseline (current 01) vs 01·B.
